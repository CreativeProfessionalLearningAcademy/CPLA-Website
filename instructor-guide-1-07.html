The Five Design Principles

The perceptual facts behind every critique
The Big Idea
Balance, contrast, alignment, repetition, and proximity are not aesthetic preferences — they are perceptual facts about how the human visual system processes information. Applied well, a design feels coherent and effortless; violated, it feels wrong even when no one can name why. The principles turn vague discomfort into specific, fixable problems.
What you're really teaching
  • Each principle has a mechanism. Balance distributes visual weight; contrast creates visibility and hierarchy through difference; alignment connects elements with shared edges; repetition builds unity and recognition; proximity groups related things and separates unrelated ones.
  • They come from perception, not fashion. Proximity works because humans automatically group nearby objects — a documented fact about perception, not a design opinion. Breaking a principle doesn't read as “unconventional”; it creates a specific, predictable problem.
  • Principles convert opinion into critique. “I don't like this” isn't actionable. “The alignment is broken — five elements float without a shared edge, so it feels chaotic” is.
  • Every principle has a signature failure you can name: bottom-heavy (balance), gray-on-gray (contrast), off-by-a-few-pixels floating (alignment), inconsistent 16/24px spacing (repetition), a caption nearer the wrong image (proximity).
  • This is the critique framework for every future module. The five principles become the shared language students use to evaluate all later work — theirs and each other's.
Scenarios & hooks to open the room
“It just doesn't look right”

The client who can't name the problem. The principles give students the vocabulary to diagnose what the client only feels.

Gray-on-gray

Body text a shade off its background. An instant, obvious contrast failure once it's named.

Off by a few pixels

A layout where elements float without a shared edge. It “feels” chaotic — alignment names why.

The false-proximity caption

A caption placed nearer the wrong image, creating a relationship that isn't true. Proximity, misused.

Teaching the lesson — step by step
Hook: name the problem 6 min

Show a subtly “off” design and ask what's wrong. Students feel it but can't name it — that gap is the lesson.

Balance and contrast 10 min

Define each with its perceptual mechanism and show applied-versus-violated pairs side by side.

Alignment, repetition, proximity 10 min

Same treatment for the remaining three; stress proximity as documented perception, not preference.

Principles as a critique framework 7 min

Turn “I don't like it” into a principle-named diagnosis on a real design, live, as a class.

Set up the application study 6 min

Ten compositions — five principles, applied and violated — plus one real-world critique.

Close on shared language 5 min

Each student critiques one object in the room using one principle by name.

Facilitating the Field Manual — Principles Application Study
Running the exercise

Students demonstrate each principle applied and violated, then critique a real design with the framework.

  • For each of the five principles, two small compositions — applied and violated — using only simple shapes and text, no photography to hide behind.
  • Keep each composition small (A6) and simple: the principle is the subject, not the artwork.
  • Label every piece “Principle: [name] — Applied/Violated,” and for each violation write one sentence naming the failure in principle vocabulary.
  • Then a real-world critique: find a design that breaks at least two principles and diagnose it with the framework.
  • Watch for “pretty versus ugly” demonstrations — require the specific named mechanism instead.
The Assignment & Deliverable — Principles Application Study
What good work looks like

Ten labeled compositions (five principles, applied and violated) plus one real-world five-principle critique.

  • Meets the bar: all ten compositions labeled, each violation named with the correct principle vocabulary.
  • Exceeds: the real-world critique cleanly separates two or more principle failures and prescribes a specific fix for each.
  • Redirect: “looks bad” gets sent back to name which principle and the mechanism behind the failure.
Watch for
  • Principles as style rules to obey. Ground each one in its perceptual mechanism, not a “do this” command.
  • Pretty-vs-ugly demonstrations. Require the specific principle isolated, not a generally nicer composition.
  • Critique stuck at “I don't like it.” Every note must carry a principle name.
  • Confusing alignment with centering, or proximity with “just spacing.” Tie each to its precise definition.
Discussion questions
  • Why is “these are perceptual facts, not opinions” such an important claim for a designer to be able to make?
  • Which principle do you think is violated most often in everyday designs, and why?
  • Can breaking a principle ever be the right call? What has to be true for that to work?
Lesson objectives
  • Define each of the five design principles and explain the perceptual mechanism underlying each one
  • Apply all five principles to evaluate a given design — identifying where each is working and where it is being violated
  • Explain why these are perceptual facts rather than stylistic preferences — grounding critique in evidence rather than opinion
  • Demonstrate each principle applied correctly and violated in a simple composition
  • Use the five principles as a professional critique framework throughout all future modules
Key vocabulary
  • Balance — the distribution of visual weight — symmetrical, asymmetrical, or (uncomfortably) neither.
  • Contrast — the degree of difference between elements — the source of visibility and hierarchy.
  • Alignment — shared edges or centerlines connecting elements — signaling intentional structure.
  • Repetition — consistent elements — colors, type, spacing — creating unity and brand recognition.
  • Proximity — grouping related elements and separating unrelated ones — communicating relationships by position.
  • Perceptual Psychology — how humans actually perceive visual information — the source of the principles' authority.
