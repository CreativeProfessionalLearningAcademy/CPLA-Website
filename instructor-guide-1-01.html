Design Is Everywhere

Learning to see intentional choices in the built world
The Big Idea
Design is intentional visual problem-solving for a specific audience — not decoration, and not art. Once students learn to ask what problem does this solve, who is it for, and what choices were made, they stop experiencing the designed world and start reading it.
What you're really teaching
  • Design vs. art is about the client, not the craft. Art answers to the maker and is free to be interpreted; design answers to an audience and a problem, and succeeds only when it communicates. This distinction is the backbone of the whole volume.
  • The word carries the idea. "Design" comes from the Latin designare — to mark out, to designate. It has meant deliberate intention from the start; decoration was never the root.
  • Wayfinding is the cleanest proof that design is invisible when it works. Harry Beck's 1933 London Underground map threw away real geography for a clean diagram of colored lines and even angles — and became the model for transit maps worldwide because it solved the rider's actual problem: which train, which direction, how many stops.
  • "Good" and "bad" are functional judgments, not taste. Don Norman's idea of the "Norman door" — a door you push when the handle says pull — shows that bad design misinforms the user. Students should learn to critique against purpose, never against personal preference.
  • The three foundational questions are the course's core tool. What problem does this solve? Who is it for? What visual choices were made? Every later lesson is a deeper answer to question three.
Scenarios & hooks to open the room
The stop sign

Why red, why an octagon, why those exact words? A shape you can read from behind, in the dark, in another language. Pure problem-solving with zero decoration.

Two remote controls

Show a cluttered 40-button remote next to a simple one. Same job, opposite choices. Ask which "respects the audience" and why.

The hospital floor

Colored lines painted on the floor to your department. Who is this for — and what happens on their worst day if it's done badly?

The cereal aisle

A kids' cereal vs. a "healthy" one. Every choice — color, character, type size — is aimed at a different audience. Nothing is accidental.

Teaching the lesson — step by step
Hook: hold up one real object 5 min

Bring a physical thing — a medicine label, a transit card, a to-go cup. Ask only: "What problem does this solve, and for whom?" Don't teach yet; just get them looking.

"Someone decided every single thing about this. Today we learn to see the deciding."

Name the distinction: design ≠ art ≠ decoration 8 min

Put the three words on the board. Design serves an audience and a problem; art serves the maker; decoration is added without a job. Anchor it and keep returning to it.

Introduce the three foundational questions 7 min

Write them where they'll stay all module. Model them out loud on your hook object so students hear the reasoning, not just the answer.

Guided practice: read a wayfinding example 10 min

Project a subway map or airport sign. Walk the class through all three questions together. Show how the "choices" answer is the richest one.

Set up the Field Manual walk 5 min

Explain the observation exercise (below) and what a strong entry looks like. Show one worked example so the bar is visible.

Close: one thing you'll never un-see 5 min

Go around: each student names one designed object they now notice differently. This cements the "design eye" as a permanent shift, not a worksheet.

Facilitating the Field Manual — The Design Eye Walk
Running the exercise

Students record designed objects and environments, applying the three questions to each.

  • Set a target of at least three objects from genuinely different categories — an object, a sign/environment, and a screen — so they don't record three of the same thing.
  • Push hardest on question three ("what choices were made"). "It's blue" is a start; "it's blue because that's the brand and it reads as trustworthy" is design thinking.
  • If you can, take the class into a hallway or outside for ten minutes. Seeing in the wild beats seeing on a slide.
  • Circulate and ask one student per object: "Would it still work if you changed that?" — the fastest way to expose an accidental vs. deliberate choice.
The Assignment & Deliverable — Design Eye Observation Log
What good work looks like

A completed log of at least three objects with all three questions answered, plus one well- and one poorly-designed everyday item, each with a reason.

  • Meets the bar: answers name a specific audience and at least one deliberate visual choice per object — not "it looks nice."
  • Exceeds: the good/bad picks are justified by purpose ("hard to read the dose in low light"), not taste ("ugly color").
  • Redirect: if every object is a logo or app, ask for one physical environment — wayfinding stretches the eye furthest.
Watch for
  • "Design means making things pretty." The single most common starting belief. Redirect relentlessly to purpose and audience.
  • Confusing design with art. Students praise a "cool" poster with no message. Ask: what is it trying to get me to do, and does it?
  • Taste-based critique. "I don't like it" isn't analysis. Require a reason tied to whether it works for its audience.
Discussion questions
  • What's something you use every day that you think is badly designed — and how would you know if you were right, not just annoyed?
  • Can something be beautiful and still be bad design? Give an example.
  • Who gets hurt when wayfinding in a hospital or airport is done poorly?
Lesson objectives
  • Define design as intentional visual problem-solving — not decoration, not art
  • Recognize designed objects and environments using the three foundational questions
  • Distinguish accidental visual choices from deliberate ones
  • Explain why design must serve an audience — and why that changes everything
  • Begin building a vocabulary for analyzing designed objects
Key vocabulary
  • Design — intentional arrangement of visual elements to communicate or solve a problem for a specific audience.
  • Visual Communication — using image, type, color, and layout to convey information or emotion, often faster than words.
  • Audience — the specific person or group a design is made for; the primary consideration in every decision.
  • Intentionality — making deliberate, considered choices rather than defaulting to habit or accident.
  • Design Eye — the trained ability to see choices in any designed object, not just experience the result.
  • Wayfinding — design that helps people navigate physical space: signs, maps, symbols, color coding.
