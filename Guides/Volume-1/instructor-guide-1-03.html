The Design Process

How professionals move from brief to finished work
The Big Idea
Good design comes from a repeatable, brief-driven process — not from waiting for inspiration. The process is iterative, not a straight line: designers loop back when critique or testing reveals a problem. Teaching the process gives students a way to work when they're stuck.
What you're really teaching
  • The brief is the foundation of everything. Message, audience, tone, deliverables, constraints, timeline — a project without a brief has no way to know if it succeeded. Every later stage answers back to it.
  • Ideation is divergent on purpose. The point of thumbnail sketching is quantity without judgment — generate many concepts before evaluating any. J. P. Guilford's distinction between divergent (generate options) and convergent (narrow to one) thinking is the mental gear-shift students must learn to make deliberately.
  • Iteration is the engine, not a sign of failure. Designers expect to make version after version, each improved by critique. Students often think a redo means they got it "wrong" — reframe iteration as how the work gets good.
  • Critique is measured against the brief, not personal taste. "I don't like the blue" is not critique; "the blue reads as cold, and the brief asked for warm and welcoming" is. This keeps feedback professional and useful.
  • The London 2012 case study shows process enabling courage. The 2012 Olympic identity was polarizing on release, but it flowed from a clear, deliberate brief — a strong example of how a defensible process lets designers make bold, brief-driven decisions and stand behind them.
Scenarios & hooks to open the room
Rebrand the coffee shop

Pick a real local spot. Write its brief together in five minutes — message, audience, tone. Students feel how much a brief decides before any drawing.

The London 2012 debate

Show the reaction to a famous, divisive identity. Ask: could a designer defend this? What in the brief would make it the right call?

The iteration reel

Show a well-known logo's evolution across versions. Every step was a loop, not a failure. Iteration made it inevitable.

School event poster

A concrete, low-stakes brief students can actually do: promote a real upcoming event. Constraints and audience are already real.

Teaching the lesson — step by step
Hook: "where do you start?" 5 min

Ask how they'd begin designing a poster. Most say "start drawing." Use that to reveal the missing first step — the brief.

Walk the stages in order 10 min

Present the process end to end, brief to reflection, in the exact sequence and names the lesson uses. Emphasize the loop-backs — where and why designers return to an earlier stage.

Align stage names to the student lesson page; it is the source of truth for the exact list.

Read a brief together 8 min

Project a sample brief. Identify what's present, what's missing, and what questions a designer would ask before starting. This models professional judgment.

Model divergent ideation 7 min

Give one prompt and thumbnail six wildly different concepts on the board in two minutes — badly, fast, no erasing. Show that quantity-without-judgment is a skill, not chaos.

Case study: process enables courage 8 min

Run the London 2012 discussion. Tie the bold decision back to the brief. Land that a good process is what lets you defend a risky choice.

Set up the Field Manual brief 5 min

Hand out the scenario. Clarify every required field of the brief and what a complete one contains before students write their own.

Facilitating the Field Manual — Write the Brief
Running the exercise

From a given scenario, students write a complete design brief, then generate thumbnail sketches for it.

  • Insist every brief field is filled — an empty "constraints" or "timeline" line is the most common gap and the most important in real work.
  • Separate the two modes out loud: first write the brief (convergent, decisions), then thumbnail (divergent, options). Don't let them sketch a finished logo — thumbnails are small, fast, and plural.
  • Set a minimum number of thumbnails (six is a good floor) so students can't stop at their first idea.
  • Circulate and ask "what does the brief say the tone is?" to keep sketches accountable to the brief rather than to preference.
The Assignment & Deliverable — Design Brief + Ideation Sheet
What good work looks like

A complete design brief for the scenario with every field filled, paired with an ideation sheet of multiple distinct thumbnail concepts.

  • Meets the bar: all brief fields present and internally consistent; at least six thumbnails that are genuinely different, not one idea drawn six times.
  • Exceeds: the thumbnails visibly respond to the brief's stated tone and audience, and the student can point to which direction they'd develop and why.
  • Redirect: if thumbnails are all variations of one concept, send them back to diverge — the deliverable is about range, not polish.
Watch for
  • "Just start designing." Skipping the brief is the default instinct. Make the brief non-optional and show how it prevents wasted work.
  • Treating iteration as failure. Students hear "revise" as "you got it wrong." Reframe every loop as the normal way good work is made.
  • Polishing one thumbnail. The point of ideation is many rough options. A single beautiful sketch has skipped the divergent step.
  • Taste-based critique. Keep all feedback anchored to the brief, not to "I like / I don't like."
Discussion questions
  • Why write a brief at all — what goes wrong on a project that never had one?
  • When is it right to loop back to an earlier stage, and when is that just avoiding a decision?
  • The London 2012 identity was divisive. Can a design be "good" if many people dislike it? What would settle the question?
Lesson objectives
  • Name and describe all seven stages of the professional design process in sequence
  • Explain why the design process is iterative rather than linear — and what triggers a return loop
  • Read and evaluate a design brief — identifying what information is present, what is missing, and what questions to ask
  • Write a complete design brief for a given scenario using all required fields
  • Apply the London 2012 case study to explain how process enables courageous, brief-driven design decisions
Key vocabulary
  • Design Brief — A document defining the parameters of a design project: message, audience, tone, deliverables, constraints, and timeline. The foundation of every project.
  • Ideation — The divergent phase of the design process in which as many concepts as possible are generated through rapid sketching before any are evaluated or developed.
  • Iteration — The cyclical refinement of a design through successive versions, each informed by critique and testing — the primary mechanism of design improvement.
  • Critique — A structured professional practice of presenting work-in-progress for feedback, evaluated against the brief rather than personal preference.
  • Thumbnail Sketch — Small, fast, rough sketches made during ideation to explore multiple concepts quickly without committing to any single direction.
  • Deliverable — The specific file, format, or physical output that the designer is required to produce at the conclusion of a project — defined in the brief.
  • Divergent Thinking — The cognitive mode of generating many possible ideas without judgment — essential during ideation. Contrasts with convergent thinking, which narrows options toward a single solution.
  • Production Specifications — Technical parameters for a final design file: resolution, color mode, file format, bleed, trim size, and any requirements for print or digital output.
