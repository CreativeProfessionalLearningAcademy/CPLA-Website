Audience & Message

Designing for a specific someone, not everyone
The Big Idea
The most technically accomplished design fails if it speaks to the wrong audience or sends the wrong message. Before any tool comes out, two questions must be answered with precision: who is this for, and what one thing must they feel, understand, or do? Audience definition is the first act of design — specificity creates resonance; generality creates invisibility.
What you're really teaching
  • An audience profile has five dimensions: demographics, psychographics, visual vocabulary, context of encounter, and prior knowledge. Psychographics — values, fears, aspirations — usually drive more visual decisions than demographics do.
  • “Everyone” is not an audience. Designing for everyone strips out every specific choice that creates resonance, because each specific choice risks excluding part of the imaginary “everyone.” The result is generic, inoffensive, and invisible.
  • The message is one testable sentence: “After seeing this design, [audience] should [feel/understand/do] [outcome].” One thing, not three. If the list has three items, there isn't a message yet.
  • Start from the audience's visual vocabulary — the packaging, apps, and magazines they already trust. Design that feels native to their world earns trust before it's read.
  • Context of encounter sets the technical constraints. A poster in a moving subway car gives the viewer about three seconds — every decision must serve legibility at speed, not elegance at leisure.
Scenarios & hooks to open the room
The “everyone 18–65” brief

Give the deliberately vague brief and ask students to make visual choices. They stall — that's the felt cost of no audience.

The supplement statement

“Health-conscious adults 35–50 should trust this is clinically credible, not gym-bro.” One sentence that steers color, type, and imagery all at once.

The three-second subway poster

A context that forces size, contrast, and hierarchy decisions before any aesthetics.

Same product, two audiences

Design the same item for two different profiles and watch the choices diverge — proof that the audience, not the product, drives the design.

Teaching the lesson — step by step
Hook: design for everyone 6 min

Hand out a vague “everyone” brief and ask for specific visual choices. The stall is the lesson — no audience, no decisions.

Teach the five dimensions 10 min

Build one audience profile live, pushing hardest on psychographics — the layer that actually generates choices.

The message statement 8 min

Introduce the sentence template and write one together. Enforce “one thing” — cut any statement carrying three ideas.

Audience to visual decisions 8 min

From the profile and message, derive five concrete choices: color palette, type tone, image style, space treatment, composition.

Set up the exercise 5 min

Assign the full profile, three message candidates, and five justified decisions. Show one worked dimension so the bar is visible.

Close on specificity 5 min

Each student states their audience in one specific sentence. Challenge any “everyone” on the spot.

Facilitating the Field Manual — Audience Profile + One-Sentence Message
Running the exercise

Students define who the design is for and the single thing it must say — in writing, before any visual work.

  • Let students pick a real or fictional scenario: a nonprofit donation appeal, a product launch, an event poster, a local rebrand.
  • Require all five dimensions filled — an empty psychographics or context line is the usual gap and the costliest.
  • Three message candidates, then a chosen one with a reason it beats the others. One idea per sentence.
  • Five specific visual decisions that trace back to a dimension — each decision must name its audience reason.
  • Peer check: is the audience specific enough to picture one person, and is the message actually testable?
The Assignment & Deliverable — Audience Profile + One-Sentence Message
What good work looks like

A complete five-dimension profile, three message candidates, a chosen statement with rationale, and five justified decisions.

  • Meets the bar: all five dimensions present and every visual decision cites the audience reason behind it.
  • Exceeds: the chosen message is genuinely testable and the five decisions are concrete — a named palette and type tone, not “modern and clean.”
  • Redirect: any “everyone” audience or three-part message goes straight back before visual work continues.
Watch for
  • “Everyone” as an audience. The central failure of the lesson — send it back every time.
  • Three-idea message statements. If it needs “and,” it isn't one message yet.
  • Demographics-only profiles. Skipping psychographics skips where the real decisions come from.
  • Decisions with no stated reason. A visual choice that doesn't trace to the profile is decoration by another name.
Discussion questions
  • Name a product that clearly designs for a specific someone. How can you tell?
  • Why does trying to include everyone make a design weaker rather than safer?
  • When does prior knowledge change the design most — say, an expert audience versus a first-timer?
Lesson objectives
  • Build a detailed audience profile using the five dimensions: demographics, psychographics, visual vocabulary, context of encounter, and prior knowledge
  • Distill a complex design objective into a single, complete, testable one-sentence message statement
  • Explain why “everyone” is not a valid audience definition — and what it costs a design to try to speak to everyone
  • Apply audience understanding to make specific, justified visual decisions about color, type, space, and image
  • Evaluate whether a completed design serves its defined audience — and identify the most critical point of misalignment
Key vocabulary
  • Audience Profile — a detailed description of the specific person a design is for: demographics, psychographics, visual vocabulary, context, prior knowledge.
  • Psychographics — the values, beliefs, fears, and aspirations beneath demographic facts — where most design decisions come from.
  • Message Statement — one testable sentence: what the audience should feel, understand, or do after seeing the design.
  • Visual Vocabulary — the design language an audience already encounters and instinctively trusts.
  • Context of Encounter — where, when, and how the design is experienced — setting reading speed, contrast, size, and hierarchy.
  • Specificity — targeting a precise audience — the property that creates resonance rather than generic invisibility.
