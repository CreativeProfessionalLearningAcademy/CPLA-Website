Design vs. Decoration

Every element must earn its place
The Big Idea
A design decision communicates; decoration is added without a job. Experienced designers subtract — they remove anything that doesn't earn its place. Beginners add. This lesson is where students first feel that reversal.
What you're really teaching
  • The Removal Test is a decision tool, not an opinion. If you remove an element and the communication doesn't get weaker, the element was decorative. It turns "do I like this?" into "does this do work?"
  • Swiss design is the historical proof. The mid-20th-century International Typographic Style — Josef Müller-Brockmann, Armin Hofmann, and the 1957 typeface Helvetica — showed that stripping ornament to clean grids, sans-serif type, and white space made communication stronger, not colder.
  • The masters said it in one line each. Dieter Rams: "Good design is as little design as possible." Antoine de Saint-Exupéry: perfection is reached "not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away." These are memorable anchors for restraint.
  • Restraint is not the same as boring or empty. The goal isn't minimalism for its own sake — it's that every remaining element has a reason. A dense design can pass the Removal Test if every part is working.
  • Context sets the dial. A wedding invitation earns more ornament than a fire-exit sign. Teach the decoration-to-design spectrum, not a rule that decoration is bad.
Scenarios & hooks to open the room
The over-decorated slide

Show a PowerPoint slide with a photo background, three fonts, a drop shadow, and clip art. Remove elements live until the message appears. Instant "aha."

Two menus

A cluttered diner menu vs. a clean one. Which makes you find the thing you want faster? Decoration can literally cost the business a sale.

The search homepage

A famous search engine is a logo and a box. Ask what's missing and why removing everything else was the design.

Road sign vs. wedding invite

Put them side by side. Both are "good design" — but the acceptable amount of decoration is opposite. Context, not taste.

Teaching the lesson — step by step
Hook: subtract in real time 6 min

Open the cluttered slide and delete one element at a time, asking after each: "Did the message get weaker?" Stop when it's cleanest. They just watched the Removal Test.

Define the two words precisely 6 min

Design decision = has a communicative job. Decoration = added for appearance alone. Neither is automatically wrong; decoration just has to be justified.

Teach the Removal Test + the Five Justification Questions 8 min

Give them the exact procedure they'll use on paper: for each element, remove it and the ask whether communication suffered; if not, cut it.

Introduce the decoration-to-design spectrum 6 min

Draw a line: fire-exit sign at one end, ornate invitation at the other. Place several real designs on it as a class. Establish that context sets how much decoration is right.

Set up the Field Manual audit 5 min

Hand out (or have students pick) one real design. Model auditing the first element together, then release them.

Close on the reversal 5 min

Land the line: "Beginners add. Designers subtract." Ask each student for one element they'd cut from something in the room.

Facilitating the Field Manual — The Removal Test Audit
Running the exercise

Students take a real design and go element by element, applying the Removal Test and the Five Justification Questions.

  • Give them a design with a real mix — some working elements, some decorative — so the audit has stakes. A local flyer or ad works well.
  • Require a verdict per element: keep (has a job) or cut (decorative). No "maybe." The forced call is where the learning is.
  • Remind them the goal is not to cut everything — a kept decorative element is fine if they can justify it by context.
  • Watch for students cutting things they simply dislike; make them name the missing communicative job before they cut.
The Assignment & Deliverable — Decoration-to-Design Audit
What good work looks like

A marked-up analysis placing the design on the decoration-to-design spectrum, justifying or removing each element, plus a revised "subtracted" version.

  • Meets the bar: every element gets a keep/cut verdict with a one-line reason tied to the message.
  • Exceeds: the "subtracted" version is visibly cleaner and the student can defend one decorative element they chose to keep on context grounds.
  • Redirect: if a student cut everything to nothing, ask what the design still needs to do — restraint isn't erasure.
Watch for
  • "More elements = more effort = better grade." The add-more instinct is strong. Reward the reasoning behind removal, not the amount on the page.
  • Treating minimalism as the goal. The goal is justified elements, not the fewest. Don't let "clean" become a new kind of thoughtless default.
  • Cutting on taste. "I'd remove it because it's ugly" fails the test. The test is whether communication weakens.
Discussion questions
  • Name a design where decoration is doing a real job. What would be lost if you removed it?
  • Why do beginners add and experts subtract — what changes with experience?
  • Where should a fast-food menu sit on the decoration-to-design spectrum, and why?
Lesson objectives
  • Articulate the difference between a decorative choice and a design decision
  • Apply the Removal Test to any element in a composition
  • Place real designs on the decoration-to-design spectrum and explain why
  • Use the Five Design Justification Questions as a self-editing framework
  • Explain why experienced designers subtract while beginners add
Key vocabulary
  • Decoration — visual elements added for appearance without a specific communicative purpose.
  • Visual Hierarchy — arranging elements so the eye moves in the intended order, most to least important.
  • Restraint — deliberately using fewer elements, each with clear purpose, instead of filling space.
  • Removal Test — if removing an element doesn't weaken communication, it's likely decorative.
  • Swiss Design — mid-century movement of grids, sans-serif type, and white space; proof that less can communicate more.
  • Ornament — a decorative detail added for enrichment, not function; not always wrong, but always requiring justification.
